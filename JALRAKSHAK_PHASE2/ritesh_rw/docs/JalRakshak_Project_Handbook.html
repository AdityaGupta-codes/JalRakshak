Phase 1 · Complete project overview

The 12 overview questions

QuestionAnswer
1. What is it?

A web application where:

  • organisations request water from a city reservoir;
  • the water department compares four allocation rules on the same requests and applies one;
  • the system stores every decision safely;
  • it forecasts demand and flags possible leaks.
2. Real-world problemWater scarcity at the distribution level. When demand is higher than supply (example: 80,000 L asked, 50,000 L available), someone must decide who gets how much, and in which order.
3. Who faces it
  • Water department operators, who must decide.
  • Organisations that depend on supply: hospitals, schools, housing colonies, shops, factories, parks.
  • Residents, indirectly.
4. How it's handled todayAssumption, not researched Fixed schedules, habit, or roughly first come first served, often on paper or spreadsheets, with no recorded reason for each decision.
5. Limitations of that
  • FCFS ignores importance (in our sample a factory takes 20,000 L first).
  • Pure priority starves low-priority users.
  • Nobody can compare "what would another rule have done?"
  • Two operators can double-allocate.
  • No audit trail, no forecasting.
6. How we address them
  • Four rules run on identical data and are shown side by side before anything is saved.
  • Water-aware rule with a starvation guard.
  • Locked database transactions.
  • An append-only audit log.
  • Demand forecast and leak alerts.
  • A panel for each organisation.
7. Objectives

(1) Implement OS scheduling rules plus a new water-aware rule on one shared engine.

(2) Measure them honestly:

  • waiting time;
  • fairness;
  • importance-weighted delivery;
  • starvation;
  • missed deadlines.

(3) Make concurrent commits safe.

(4) Forecast demand and detect leaks.

(5) Provide secure, role-based panels.

8. Users
  • Requesters (role requester): staff of one organisation.
  • Administrators (role admin): the water department.
9. Modules
  • Scheduler (C++).
  • Allocation service.
  • Auth.
  • User panel and admin panel.
  • ML pipeline.
  • Database.
  • Experiments.
10. Why usefulMakes a hard trade-off visible and measurable, and makes the decision safe and accountable.
11. Limitations
  • Synthetic data.
  • One reservoir, one pipeline.
  • Soft deadlines.
  • Hand-chosen weights.
  • Water-aware is not best on every measure.
  • No HTTPS, email reset, 2-factor sign-in, connection pool or backups.
12. Scope

In scope:

  • allocation decisions for one reservoir;
  • comparison of rules;
  • safe storage;
  • forecasting on generated data;
  • two panels.

Out of scope:

  • physical pumps and valves;
  • billing;
  • real sensors;
  • pipe networks;
  • multiple reservoirs.

A. 30-second explanation

"JalRakshak decides how a reservoir's limited water is shared when demand is higher than supply.

  • We treated it like CPU scheduling: each request is a process, the pipeline is the CPU.
  • We implemented four schedulers in C++, including our own Water-aware rule that never leaves anyone with zero water.
  • PostgreSQL stores every decision, and its row locks make sure two operators can never hand out the same water twice.
  • Organisations request water in their own panel; the department compares the rules and applies one."

B. 2-minute explanation

"When a reservoir has 50,000 litres but users ask for 80,000, someone must decide who gets what. Usually it's first come, first served, which can let a factory take water while a hospital waits.

We modelled this as an OS scheduling problem:

  • Each request is like a process, with an arrival time, a priority, an urgency and a deadline.
  • The supply pipeline is like a CPU delivering 500 litres a minute, so a 10,000-litre request has a 'burst time' of 20 minutes.

Our C++ scheduler implements four rules on one shared simulation engine:

  • first come, first served;
  • non-preemptive priority;
  • round robin with a 2,000-litre quantum;
  • our own Water-aware rule.

Water-aware scores each request on priority, urgency, scarcity, population and past fairness. It caps each grant so everyone still waiting keeps at least 30% of their request, which prevents starvation.

The FastAPI backend reads the requests from PostgreSQL, runs the scheduler as a child process, and saves the plan in one transaction. It locks the reservoir row first, so two operators clicking at once can't double-allocate. We proved this with an experiment: with locks, 80,000 litres were allocated; without them, 160,000.

An ML module forecasts demand with about 5% error on our generated data, and flags leaks. Organisations have a user panel to request water and report problems; the department has an admin panel with an audit log.

Our honest finding: no rule wins everything. Round robin is fairest, priority serves important users most, and Water-aware was the only rule that never left anyone with nothing in our tests."

C. Detailed technical explanation (viva examiner)

  1. Data model.
    • water_requests belong to users (organisations), which belong to zones.
    • A committed plan is one scheduler_runs row plus one allocations row per request.
    • Integrity is enforced by the schema, e.g. CHECK (available_litres >= 0) and CHECK (available_litres <= capacity_litres).
  2. Commit path: service.commit_allocation() runs as one transaction:
    • it locks the reservoir row (SELECT … FOR UPDATE);
    • it locks the pending requests (FOR UPDATE OF r);
    • it builds the JSON input;
    • it starts the C++ binary with subprocess.run(), passing JSON through stdin and reading the plan from stdout (pipes, 10-second timeout);
    • it inserts the run and allocations, updates request statuses, subtracts the exact total from the reservoir, and inserts a notification for each organisation plus one audit row;
    • then COMMIT. Any exception → ROLLBACK.
  3. Scheduler internals.
    • run_sequential() is a discrete-event simulation: a clock and the remaining water. A picker function chooses which ready request goes next.
    • FCFS, Priority and Water-aware differ only in the picker. Round Robin has its own queue loop.
    • execute() rounds grants down to 0.01 L, checks invariants, then computes metrics.
  4. Water-aware.
    • The score is a weighted sum of 5 factors, each scaled to 0–1:
      • priority 0.30;
      • urgency 0.25;
      • scarcity 0.15;
      • population 0.15;
      • fairness 0.15.
    • Cap = max(0.3 × asked, water_left − 0.3 × still_asked_by_others).
  5. Concurrency proof. 4 threads commit at the same instant (tests/concurrency_demo.py).
    • With locks: 1 plan saved, 80,000 L.
    • Without locks: 2 plans saved, 160,000 L.
  6. Security.
    • PBKDF2-SHA256 password hashes, 600,000 iterations.
    • Random session tokens, of which only the SHA-256 hash is stored; they expire after 12 h.
    • Lockout after 5 wrong passwords.
    • Role checks on every endpoint.
    • Append-only audit log enforced by a trigger.
  7. ML.
    • Leak detection: robust z-score with median and MAD (median absolute deviation).
    • Forecasting: 4 models compared on 90 held-out days. Random Forest wins: MAE (mean absolute error) 1,223 L (5.1%), against 1,849 L for the baseline.
    • Today's forecast feeds the scarcity factor.

Journey 2: the administrator allocates

  1. The admin signs in. The first time, ForcedPasswordChange makes them choose a new password.
  2. Allocate tab → POST /allocate/simulate → all 4 rules run. Nothing is written to the database.
  3. The admin compares the metric cards and clicks Apply on one rule, twice to confirm → POST /allocate/commit → the locked transaction described above.

Phase 1 wrap-up

Summary.

  • JalRakshak is a decision-support web app for sharing scarce water, built on an OS scheduling analogy.
  • The OS part we implemented ourselves is the scheduling simulation, plus the use of child processes, pipes and locks.
  • The DBMS part is substantial: constraints, transactions, locks, a trigger and indexes.

Remember:

  • request = process; pipeline = CPU; burst = litres ÷ 500;
  • simulate (no save) vs commit (one locked transaction);
  • guarantee floor = starvation guard;
  • requester vs admin;
  • "implemented by us" vs "provided by OS/DBMS".

Check questions (open each after you answer):

1. What is the burst time of a 6,000 L request?12 minutes, because the pipeline delivers 500 L per minute: 6,000 ÷ 500 = 12. In code: finish = start + grant / flow_rate_lpm in sequential.cpp.
2. Simulate vs commit: which one changes the database?Only commit. simulate() just reads requests and runs all 4 rules. commit_allocation() locks rows and writes the run, allocations, statuses, the new water level, notifications and the audit row in one transaction.
3. Did we implement paging or virtual memory?No. Answer: "Our program runs on an OS that uses virtual memory, but our code doesn't implement or control paging. The OS concept we implemented ourselves is scheduling, as a simulation, plus process creation, pipes and locking used through the OS and DBMS."
4. What does the guarantee floor solve, and what is its OS name?It prevents starvation (a request getting nothing). In OS terms it plays the role that aging plays in priority scheduling: low-priority work can't be ignored forever.
5. Who decides priority, and when does a request receive water?The admin sets each organisation's priority (Settings tab, users.priority; default by type, e.g. hospital 5). A request receives water only when an admin applies a plan (POST /allocate/commit). Until then it stays pending.

C++17

1 DefinitionC++ is a compiled, statically-typed language. "17" is the 2017 version of the standard (gives std::optional, structured bindings, etc.).
2 CategoryProgramming language (systems language).
3 Why hereThe OS-subject part, the schedulers, is written in the language OS courses use. It's fast and deterministic: same input, same output (tested: "all algorithms deterministic").
4 Problem solvedRuns each allocation rule on the request list and returns a plan plus metrics, with strict invariants (never more than available, never more than asked).
5 How it worksg++ translates source to machine code once. The binary then runs directly on the CPU with no interpreter. Memory is handled by STL containers (std::vector, std::deque, std::map) and std::unique_ptr, which free memory automatically (RAII). There is no manual new/delete.
6 Wherescheduler/
7 Files / functions
  • main.cpp: main()
  • scheduler.cpp: make_scheduler(), execute(), check_invariants(), compute_metrics()
  • sequential.cpp: run_sequential()
  • fcfs.cpp, priority.cpp, round_robin.cpp, water_aware.cpp: classes deriving from Scheduler
8 CommunicationReads one JSON document from stdin and writes one to stdout. Exit code 0 means OK; 1 means error JSON.
9 If removedNo allocation plans. The backend raises SchedulerUnavailable ("scheduler binary not found") → HTTP 500.
10 Alternatives
  • Python: simpler, one language less, but slower and less "OS-like".
  • Rust: safer, but the team would need to learn it.
  • Java: needs a JVM.
11 Pros / cons+ speed, control, matches the OS subject, small static binary. − compile step, harder to write, a second language to maintain.
12 Viva point"Each scheduler is a class implementing the abstract Scheduler interface (polymorphism). make_scheduler() is a factory function."

PostgreSQL 16 (with SQL and PL/pgSQL)

1 DefinitionAn open-source object-relational DBMS. SQL is its query language; PL/pgSQL is its built-in procedural language for functions and triggers.
2 CategoryRDBMS (database server).
3 WhyACID transactions, row-level locks, CHECK constraints, triggers, JSONB, advisory locks. All of these are DBMS-syllabus topics we actually use.
4 ProblemData that must stay correct when many users change it at the same time.
5 How
  • A server process accepts connections and starts one backend process per connection.
  • Data lives in 8 KB pages on disk, cached in shared memory.
  • Changes are first written to the WAL (write-ahead log) for durability.
  • MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets readers see a consistent snapshot without blocking writers.
  • Row locks (FOR UPDATE) make writers wait for each other.

Phase 2 wrap-up

Summary. There are four runtime pieces:

  • browser app (React, served by nginx);
  • API (FastAPI on Uvicorn);
  • scheduler binary (C++);
  • database (PostgreSQL).

There is also a one-shot ML job. Docker Compose wires them together. Each technology has a clear job, and an honest alternative exists for each.

Remember:

  • framework calls you, library is called by you;
  • Uvicorn = server, FastAPI = framework;
  • psycopg2 = driver;
  • Depends() = dependency injection;
  • three validation layers;
  • multi-stage Docker build;
  • CORS because port 3000 ≠ port 8000.
1. Why is FastAPI a framework but psycopg2 a library?FastAPI calls our endpoint functions when requests arrive (inversion of control). We call psycopg2's functions ourselves when we want to run SQL.
2. How does the C++ scheduler get into the backend container?Stage 1 of backend/Dockerfile compiles it in gcc:13; COPY --from=scheduler-build puts the binary into the python:3.12-slim image at /usr/local/bin/jalrakshak-scheduler.
3. Name the three validation layers.
  1. Pydantic models in schemas.py.
  2. Business rules in the service code (e.g. at most 5 pending requests, request ≤ capacity).
  3. PostgreSQL CHECK, FK, UNIQUE and NOT NULL constraints.
4. Why does the backend need CORS?The page is served from localhost:3000 but calls localhost:8000. Different ports are different origins, so browsers block the call unless the server allows it (CORSMiddleware, CORS_ORIGINS).
5. What would break if the ML container never ran?Nothing essential. Forecast and leak tabs would be empty, and the scheduler's scarcity factor would use the litres still requested instead of the forecast (_forecast_today() returns None). Allocation still works.

Code section 1: the shared simulation engine, run_sequential()

cpp// scheduler/src/sequential.cpp (shortened)
std::vector<Allocation> run_sequential(const Input& in, const Picker& pick) {
    std::vector<bool> done(n, false);
    SeqState st;
    st.clock = initial_clock(in);            // first arrival + batch window
    st.remaining = in.available_litres;
    for (size_t handled = 0; handled < n; ++handled) {
        st.pending_litres = /* sum of litres still asked by not-done requests */;
        std::vector<size_t> ready = collect_ready();     // arrived && !done
        if (ready.empty()) { st.clock = next_arrival; ready = collect_ready(); }  // idle pipeline
        Pick p = pick(ready, st);                        // <-- the only thing that differs per algorithm
        done[p.index] = true;
        double grant = std::min(r.requested_litres, st.remaining);
        if (p.max_grant) grant = std::min(grant, *p.max_grant);          // Water-aware cap
        if (grant <= kEps || grant < min_allocation_ratio * requested) -> "rejected", 0 L
        else { start = st.clock; finish = start + grant / flow_rate_lpm;
               wait = start - arrival; st.remaining -= grant; st.clock = finish; }
    }
}
B Purpose
Simulate one pipeline serving requests one after another, non-preemptively, for any "who goes next" rule.
C Key lines
initial_clock applies the batch window (backend default "all" = wait until the last pending request has arrived). collect_ready is the ready queue. The picker returns an index, a score and an optional cap. grant / flow_rate_lpm is the burst time. A grant smaller than 20% of the request is refused (min_allocation_ratio), so nobody gets a useless trickle.
D Concepts
Discrete-event simulation, ready queue, non-preemptive scheduling, burst time, waiting time = start − arrival, higher-order functions (the picker is a lambda), the strategy pattern.
E Flow
loop n times → compute pending litres → ready set (or jump the clock forward if idle) → pick → grant → advance clock.
F Input / output
In: Input (requests, water, config) + a picker. Out: one Allocation per request (litres, start, finish, wait, status).
G Interaction
Called by FCFS, Priority and Water-aware. Its result goes to execute() for rounding, invariant checks and metrics.
H Why this way
One engine means the comparison is fair: the rules differ only in their choice, not in how time and water are counted.
I If removed / changed
Each algorithm would need its own loop: duplicated, bug-prone code, and results that might not be comparable.
J Improvement
Use a priority queue (heap) for O(n log n) instead of scanning the ready list (O(n²)). Fine at our scale, slow for 100,000 requests.

def run_scheduler(payload, binary=None, timeout=None):
    binary = binary or config.SCHEDULER_BIN
    timeout = timeout or config.SCHEDULER_TIMEOUT_S            # 10 s
    try:
        proc = subprocess.run([binary], input=json.dumps(payload), capture_output=True,
                              text=True, timeout=timeout)
    except FileNotFoundError:
        raise SchedulerUnavailable(f"scheduler binary not found: {binary}")
    except subprocess.TimeoutExpired:
        raise SchedulerUnavailable(f"scheduler timed out after {timeout}s")
    data = json.loads(proc.stdout)       # (JSONDecodeError -> SchedulerUnavailable)
    if proc.returncode != 0:
        raise SchedulerError(data.get("error", "scheduler failed"))
    return data

Phase 4 · Operating Systems concepts: deep dive

The golden rule for your viva. Separate three things:

  1. Our code explicitly implements an OS algorithm. Example: FCFS / Priority / Round Robin as a simulation of water delivery in C++. Explicit
  2. Our code asks the OS to do something. Example: create a child process, open pipes, create threads. Indirect
  3. Our program merely runs on an OS, which does paging, CPU scheduling of real processes, and so on. Theoretical, since we don't implement or control it.

Never say "our project implements paging". Say: "it runs on Linux, which uses paging; we didn't implement memory management."

A. Process management

Program vs process Indirect

1 Definition
A program is a file of instructions on disk (/usr/local/bin/jalrakshak-scheduler). A process is a program while it is running: it has memory, open files, a process ID (PID) and a state.
2 Why needed
The OS must track each running instance separately. Two admins clicking "Compare" at the same moment start two separate scheduler processes from the same program file.
3 Internals
The kernel creates a process structure (the PCB), gives it a virtual address space, loads the code, sets up file descriptors 0/1/2 and puts it in the ready queue.
4 In our project
The backend (Uvicorn/Python) is one long-running process. Each scheduler run is a short-lived child process. PostgreSQL runs one server process per client connection. Each Docker container's main program is PID 1 inside its namespace.
5 Where
scheduler_client.run_scheduler(); backend/Dockerfile CMD.
6 Example
Simulate with 4 algorithms → _plan() is called 4 times → 4 child processes are created one after another, each living a few milliseconds.
7 Analogy
A recipe book (program) vs a cook actually cooking from it (process). Four cooks can follow the same recipe at once.
9 Pros / limits
+ isolation: a crash in the C++ can't corrupt the API. − creating a process costs time (milliseconds).
10 Viva
Q: Is the scheduler a thread or a process? A: A separate process, created by subprocess.run for each run. It shares no memory with the API and talks only through pipes.

Process vs thread, and multithreading Indirect (in the app) · Explicit (in tests)

1 Definition
A thread is a path of execution inside a process. Threads share the process's memory; processes don't share memory.
2 Why
A web server must serve several users at once. While one request waits for the database, another can run.
3 Internals
Linux schedules threads like processes (both are "tasks"). In CPython, the GIL allows one thread to run Python code at a time, but it is released during I/O (socket reads, waiting for a child process).
4 Ours
FastAPI runs every def endpoint in a thread pool (Starlette/AnyIO). Ours are all def, so two admins committing at once are two threads, each with its own DB connection. The tests create threads explicitly: threading.Thread ×4 with a threading.Barrier(4) to start them at the same instant.

A process is a kitchen; threads are cooks in the same kitchen sharing the fridge. Separate processes are separate kitchens.
9 Pros / limits

1 Definition
Serve the highest-priority ready request; once started, it isn't interrupted.
3 Internals
Picker: highest priority, then earlier arrival, then lower id.
4–5 Ours

Round Robin Explicit

1 Definition
Each ready job gets a fixed time quantum, then goes to the back of the queue; preemptive by time.
3 Internals
A std::deque queue. Each turn grants min(quantum, still needed, water left), advances the clock, admits new arrivals, and re-queues the request if it's unfinished. New arrivals join before the request that was just served rejoins.
4–5 Ours
round_robin.cpp, class RoundRobin; quantum = quantum_litres = 2,000 L (= 4 minutes at 500 L/min). min_allocation_ratio is not applied, because water already delivered can't be taken back.
6 Example
Gantt above; Jain fairness 0.91, the highest.
7 Analogy
Passing one microphone around a group: everyone speaks for 30 seconds per turn.
9 Pros / limits
+ fair, shortest response times, no starvation. − ignores importance; many partial grants; quantum choice matters (too small = many switches; too large = FCFS). We reject absurdly small quanta (test_round_robin_rejects_absurd_quantum).
10 Viva
Q: What happens if the quantum is very large? A: RR becomes FCFS, since each request finishes in its first turn.

Water-aware (our own) Explicit

Covered in Phase 3, code section 2. In OS terms it is a dynamic, multi-factor priority scheduler with an anti-starvation reservation:

  • The dynamic part: deadline pressure grows as the clock advances, and scarcity changes as water is used.
  • Its "reservation" (the floor) has no direct textbook equivalent. It is closest to proportional-share ideas (each user is guaranteed a minimum share).

Measured across 600 scenarios: 0 requests left with nothing at moderate and severe scarcity. Not the fastest; not the fairest; best importance-weighted delivery only at moderate scarcity.

SJF (Shortest Job First) Not implemented

SJF picks the smallest burst (smallest litres) first. It minimises average waiting time, but can starve big jobs. It's easy to add: a new picker choosing the minimum requested_litres, about 20 lines like fcfs.cpp. If an examiner asks, say so honestly. You can also point out that Water-aware's scarcity factor favours smaller requests when water is scarce, a partial SJF-like effect.

H. Deadlock management

1 Definition
A set of processes, each waiting for a resource held by another in the set, so none can proceed.
2 Four necessary conditions (Coffman)
  1. Mutual exclusion: row locks are exclusive.
  2. Hold and wait: a transaction holds the reservoir lock while waiting for request locks.
  3. No preemption: a lock can't be taken away.
  4. Circular wait: T1 waits for T2, which waits for T1.
All four must hold at once for deadlock.
3 Prevention (ours)
Partial. We break circular wait with a consistent lock order: every allocation transaction locks the reservoir row first, then the request rows (ORDER BY arrival_time, request_id). Two commits can never hold locks in opposite orders. A second commit simply waits at the reservoir lock.
4 Avoidance
Not implemented. The Banker's algorithm (grant only if the system stays in a "safe state") isn't used. It needs advance knowledge of maximum needs, which is impractical for a database.
5 Detection and recovery
Indirect. PostgreSQL runs a deadlock detector after deadlock_timeout (default 1 s): it builds a wait-for graph, finds a cycle, and aborts one transaction (error 40P01). Our code would then roll back and return HTTP 500 (no automatic retry).
6 Where a deadlock could appear
A user cancelling request #5 (locks request #5) while an admin commit (holding the reservoir lock) tries to lock request #5. The commit just waits for the cancel to finish. The cancel never asks for the reservoir lock, so there is no cycle. Reasoned, not tested
7 Analogy
Two cars at a narrow bridge each waiting for the other. A rule like "always enter from the north first" removes the circle.
10 Viva
Q: Which Coffman condition do you break? A: Circular wait, by ordering locks: reservoir, then requests. Follow-up: What if a deadlock happens anyway? PostgreSQL detects it, aborts one transaction; we roll back cleanly; the user can retry.
  Deadlock (avoided):                    Our order (no cycle possible):
  T1 holds A, waits B                    T1: lock reservoir -> lock requests -> ... COMMIT
  T2 holds B, waits A   (cycle)          T2: lock reservoir (WAIT until T1 commits) -> ...

Phase 4 wrap-up

Summary. What we implemented:

  • scheduling policies, metrics and starvation prevention (as a water simulation);
  • mutual exclusion through DB row locks, a mutex and advisory locks;
  • deadlock prevention through lock ordering.

What we use from the OS: process creation, pipes, exit codes, threads, system calls, isolation. What is only theoretical: paging, virtual memory, shared memory.

Remember:

  • the three-way distinction (explicit / indirect / theoretical);
  • Gantt charts of the 4 rules;
  • Coffman conditions and which one we break;
  • RR wait = response time;
  • SJF not implemented;
  • RAII.
1. In Round Robin, is our wait_time the total waiting time?No. It's the time until the first turn (response time). Total waiting would also include the gaps between later turns.
2. Which Coffman condition does our design break, and how?Circular wait. All allocation transactions lock the reservoir row first, then the request rows in a fixed order, so no cycle of waits can form.
3. Name two system calls that happen when the backend runs the scheduler.Examples: pipe2 (create pipes), clone/execve or posix_spawn (start the child), write/read (send and receive JSON), wait4 (collect the exit status). They happen inside Python's subprocess, not in our code directly.
4. Why can FCFS starve requests in our project but not on a CPU?CPU time is renewable: every process eventually gets a turn. Water is consumed: once 50,000 L are given away, later requests get nothing.
5. Why use a separate process for the scheduler instead of a thread?Isolation (a crash can't take down the API), language independence (C++ vs Python), a simple, testable interface (pipes + JSON), and a timeout can kill it cleanly.

Strong and weak entities

Strong entities have their own identity: zones, reservoirs, users, accounts. Weak-like entities depend on a parent for meaning:

  • allocations (meaningless without its run and request; naturally identified by (run_id, request_id));
  • sessions, notifications (deleted with their account: ON DELETE CASCADE).

We gave them surrogate PKs anyway (simpler joins). Strictly speaking that makes them strong entities in the schema, with the dependency enforced by FKs and UNIQUE.

Converting ER to tables (the rules we followed)

  1. Each entity becomes a table; its key becomes the PK.
  2. A 1:N relationship becomes an FK on the "N" side (e.g. water_requests.user_id).
  3. An M:N relationship becomes a new table holding both FKs (allocations), plus the relationship's own attributes (allocated_litres, wait_minutes, score).
  4. A multivalued attribute becomes a separate table (daily consumption per zone → consumption).

  • B-tree: the default PostgreSQL index. It is a balanced tree (a B+-tree variant): leaf pages hold sorted keys with pointers (TIDs) to table rows, and lookups take O(log n).
  • Clustered vs non-clustered: PostgreSQL tables are heaps (unordered), so every index is effectively non-clustered/secondary. The CLUSTER command can reorder a table once, but doesn't maintain the order. We don't use it.

Concept 1: Process, child process and pipes

1 Definition
A process is a running program. A child process is one started by another process (its parent). A pipe is a one-way channel: what one process writes, the other reads.
2 Analogy
You (parent) call a plumber (child). You pass the problem through a letterbox (stdin pipe); the plumber passes the bill back through another slot (stdout pipe) and gives a thumbs-up or thumbs-down (exit code).
3 Technical
The kernel gives each process its own memory and file-descriptor table. A pipe is a kernel buffer with a read end and a write end. When the parent creates the child, it connects the pipe ends to the child's fd 0 (stdin) and fd 1 (stdout). The parent later waits to collect the exit status.
4 In JalRakshak
Every simulate or commit starts jalrakshak-scheduler as a child of the Python backend.
5 Code
proc = subprocess.run([binary], input=json.dumps(payload), capture_output=True, text=True, timeout=10)
6 Steps
  1. Python creates pipes.
  2. The kernel starts the child running the binary.
  3. Python writes JSON and closes stdin.
  4. The child reads until end of input, computes, writes JSON, and returns 0.
  5. Python reads stdout, collects the exit status, and parses the result.
7 Mistakes
Saying "we implemented fork()" (we didn't; the library did). Forgetting the timeout (a stuck child would hang the request while locks are held). Using shell=True (injection risk).
8 Vs similar
Thread: shares memory, same process. Process: separate memory. Pipe vs socket: a pipe connects related processes on one machine; a socket can connect any processes, even across a network (we use sockets for HTTP and the DB).
9 Practice
What exit code does the scheduler return for unknown command-line flags, and why does it differ from an input error? (Answer: 2 for usage errors, 1 for input/processing errors with error JSON. This lets the caller tell "called wrongly" apart from "bad data".)
10 Viva
Q: Why is a separate process safer than calling C++ code inside Python? A: If the C++ crashes or loops, only the child is affected. The parent kills it after 10 s and rolls back, and the API stays up.

Concept 2: Starvation and aging (our guarantee floor)

1 Definition
Starvation: a job waits forever (or, for water, gets nothing). Aging: gradually increasing the priority of waiting jobs so they eventually run.
2 Analogy
At a wedding buffet the VIPs go first; if VIPs keep arriving, regular guests never eat. Rule: "everyone gets at least one plate reserved".
3 Technical
Static-priority schedulers starve low-priority work when high-priority work keeps coming. Fixes: aging (priority grows with waiting time), fair-share scheduling, reservations. For a consumable resource, a reservation is needed: the water must be held back before it's used up.
4 In JalRakshak
Priority leaves 3 of 8 with nothing; Water-aware leaves 0. Deadline pressure grows as time passes (aging-like), and the floor reserves 30% for everyone still waiting.
5 Code
cap = max(floor × asked, water_left − floor × others_still_asking) in water_aware.cpp
6 Steps (sample)
  • After the hospital, Shanti and Rajpur are served, 13,000 L is left.
  • The others ask 37,000 L in total; 30% of that = 11,100 L is reserved.
  • The school can take at most 13,000 − 11,100 = 1,900 L.
  • Each later request gets about its 30%.
  • Final: no zeros.
7 Mistakes
Saying "Round Robin has no starvation, so it's best". It also ignores importance (the hospital gets only 70%). Also, a floor too high (0.5) makes everything equal and ignores priority.
8 Vs similar
Starvation (someone waits forever while others progress) vs deadlock (nobody progresses) vs livelock (everyone is busy but nothing gets done).
9 Practice
If the floor were 0, what would Water-aware become? (Pure score order: the most unequal, closest to Priority. Experiments: 4.29 requests left with nothing under severe scarcity.)
10 Viva
Q: Is the floor the same as aging? A: Same purpose (prevent starvation), different mechanism: aging changes the order over time; the floor reserves a minimum amount, which a consumable resource needs.

Concept 3: Race condition and critical section

1 Definition
A race condition is a bug where the result depends on timing between concurrent operations. A critical section is the code that must run by one at a time to avoid it.
2 Analogy
Two people book the last train seat at the same second on two counters. Both see "1 seat free"; both get a ticket.
3 Technical
The classic pattern is read → decide → write. If two actors interleave between read and write, one decision is based on stale data. Fix: mutual exclusion (locks) around read-decide-write, or atomic operations.
4 In JalRakshak
Two admins applying plans. Also: login failure counters, the "max 5 pending" check, the schema upgrade.
5 Code
SELECT reservoir_id, available_litres FROM reservoirs WHERE reservoir_id = %s FOR UPDATE
6 Steps
Without the lock:
  • T1 reads 8 pending; T2 reads 8 pending.
  • Both plan; both write → double allocation (demo: 160,000 L).
With the lock:
  • T2 waits at the SELECT until T1 commits.
  • Then T2 reads 0 pending → 409.
7 Mistakes
Thinking a CHECK constraint is enough (it only stops negative water). Locking in Python only (doesn't work across processes or machines). Holding locks during slow work unnecessarily.
8 Vs similar
Race condition (the bug) vs critical section (the protected region) vs mutex (the tool) vs atomicity (all-or-nothing, a different guarantee: a transaction can be atomic and still race without locks).
9 Practice
Why does auth.login() lock the account row? (Two parallel wrong guesses could both read 4 failures and both write 5, so the lockout could be skipped or miscounted.)
10 Viva
Q: Where does your critical section start and end? A: From the FOR UPDATE on the reservoir row to COMMIT/ROLLBACK at the end of the with conn: block in commit_allocation().

Concept 4: Deadlock

1 Definition
Two or more processes each wait for something another holds, so none can continue.
2 Analogy
Two people in a corridor, each politely waiting for the other to move first, forever.
3 Technical
It needs all four Coffman conditions: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, circular wait. Strategies: prevention (break a condition), avoidance (Banker's algorithm), detection + recovery (wait-for graph, abort a victim).
4 In JalRakshak
Prevention: a fixed lock order (reservoir → requests). Detection: PostgreSQL's built-in deadlock detector (error 40P01).
5 Code
In commit_allocation(): first … reservoirs … FOR UPDATE, then _fetch_pending(cur, rid, lock=True) (FOR UPDATE OF r, ordered by arrival and id).
6 Steps
  • T1 locks the reservoir.
  • T2 requests the reservoir and waits (it holds nothing yet, so there's no cycle).
  • T1 locks the requests, writes, commits.
  • T2 proceeds.
7 Mistakes
Claiming "deadlocks are impossible". Rather: "we prevent the common cycle, and the DBMS detects any other".
8 Vs similar
Deadlock (stuck forever) vs blocking (normal waiting that ends when the other finishes).
9 Practice
Which Coffman condition would you break by having transactions request all locks at once? (Hold and wait.)
10 Viva
Q: What happens to the user if PostgreSQL aborts their transaction because of a deadlock? A: Rollback, nothing saved; HTTP 500 with the database error; they can retry. Automatic retry is a possible improvement.

Concept 5: Transactions and ACID

1 Definition
A transaction is a group of database operations treated as one unit: either all happen or none do.
2 Analogy
A bank transfer: debit A and credit B. Debit without credit is unacceptable.
3 Technical
  • Atomicity: undo on failure.
  • Consistency: constraints hold.
  • Isolation: concurrent transactions don't see each other's partial work.
  • Durability: once committed, the change survives crashes (the WAL is written to disk first).
4 In JalRakshak
Applying a plan writes 6 kinds of rows. All of them or none.
5 Code
with conn: + with conn.cursor() as cur:. psycopg2 begins on the first statement, commits at the end of the block, and rolls back on an exception.
6 Steps
BEGIN → lock → plan → INSERT run → INSERT allocations → UPDATE requests → UPDATE reservoir → INSERT notifications → INSERT audit → COMMIT. Any failure → ROLLBACK.
7 Mistakes
Thinking "atomic" means "isolated". Committing after each statement (autocommit) inside a multi-step change.
8 Vs similar
Transaction (unit of work) vs lock (concurrency tool used inside transactions) vs constraint (a rule checked at statement time).
9 Practice
If inserting notifications fails after the reservoir was updated, what is in the DB? (Nothing from this attempt: everything is rolled back, and the water level is unchanged.)
10 Viva
Q: Which ACID property does PostgreSQL give you without any code? A: Durability (WAL), and atomicity once we define the transaction boundary. Isolation needs our locks for correctness in this case.

Concept 6: Isolation levels and READ COMMITTED

1 Definition
An isolation level decides how much one transaction can see of others' changes.
2 Analogy
Exam hall rules: can you see a classmate's answer only after they submit (committed), or even while they're writing (uncommitted)?
3 Technical
The SQL standard defines four levels: READ UNCOMMITTED, READ COMMITTED, REPEATABLE READ, SERIALIZABLE. PostgreSQL treats READ UNCOMMITTED as READ COMMITTED (no dirty reads). In READ COMMITTED, each statement sees data committed before it started. A statement waiting on a row lock re-checks the newest version of that row.
4 In JalRakshak
The default level is used. Correctness comes from FOR UPDATE locks plus that re-check.
5 Code
SHOW default_transaction_isolation; → read committed (checked on the test server).
6 Steps
  • T2 waits on a request row locked by T1.
  • T1 commits status = 'approved'.
  • T2 re-evaluates WHERE status = 'pending' on the new version, which no longer matches.
  • The row is skipped, so T2 sees 0 pending.
7 Mistakes
Believing READ COMMITTED alone prevents lost updates (it doesn't without locks). Believing SERIALIZABLE is "free" (it causes aborts that need retries).
8 Vs similar
Isolation level (visibility policy) vs lock (explicit mutual exclusion) vs MVCC (the versioning mechanism behind visibility).
9 Practice
Which anomaly is allowed in our commit and harmless? (Phantom: a new request inserted during a commit isn't planned and stays pending.)
10 Viva
Q: Why not SERIALIZABLE? A: Row locks already serialise the conflicting part. SERIALIZABLE would add abort-and-retry handling with no extra safety for this workload.

Concept 7: Functional dependency and normalisation

1 Definition
A functional dependency X → Y means X's value determines Y's value. Normalisation organises tables so each fact is stored once.
2 Analogy
Your roll number determines your name. If your name is written in 20 registers and you change it, you must update all 20 (an update anomaly).
3 Technical
  • 1NF: atomic values.
  • 2NF: no partial dependency on a composite key.
  • 3NF: no transitive dependency (non-key → non-key).
  • BCNF: every determinant is a candidate key.
4 In JalRakshak
zone_id → population is stored only in zones; requests reach it by join.
5 Code
JOIN zones z ON z.zone_id = u.zone_id in PENDING_SQL instead of copying the population into each request.
6 Steps
List the FDs → find the keys → check each non-key column depends on "the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key".
7 Mistakes
Calling every redundancy a bug. Our allocations.reservoir_id and audit_log.actor are deliberate denormalisations with reasons.
8 Vs similar
Normalisation (remove redundancy) vs denormalisation (add controlled redundancy for speed or history).
9 Practice
If we stored zone_population in water_requests, which anomaly appears? (An update anomaly: changing the zone population would require updating every request, and old requests could disagree.)
10 Viva
Q: Is users in 3NF given user_type and priority? A: Yes. user_type doesn't determine priority (the admin can change it per organisation), so there's no transitive FD.

Concept 8: Index (B-tree)

1 Definition
A separate sorted structure that lets the DBMS find rows without scanning the whole table.
2 Analogy
The index at the back of a textbook: look up "deadlock" → page 214, instead of reading every page.
3 Technical
A balanced tree of pages; leaves hold sorted keys and row pointers (TIDs); lookup O(log n). It must be updated on every insert or update (write cost). The planner decides whether using it is cheaper than a sequential scan.
4 In JalRakshak
idx_requests_status finds pending requests; accounts_email_key on lower(email) enables case-insensitive login and uniqueness.
5 Code
CREATE INDEX idx_requests_status ON water_requests(status);
6 Steps (real EXPLAIN)
Bitmap Index Scan on idx_requests_status → Bitmap Heap Scan on water_requests → joins via users_pkey, zones_pkey → Sort.
7 Mistakes
Indexing every column (slows writes). Expecting an index on a 10-row table to be used (a sequential scan is cheaper there).
8 Vs similar
Index vs key: a key is a logical rule (uniqueness, reference); an index is a physical structure (PostgreSQL creates one automatically for each PK and UNIQUE).
9 Practice
Why lower(email) rather than email? (So lookup and uniqueness ignore case: "A@x.com" equals "a@x.com".)
10 Viva
Q: Clustered index in PostgreSQL? A: PostgreSQL tables are heaps. All indexes are secondary; CLUSTER reorders a table once but doesn't maintain it.

Concept 9: Password hashing and session tokens

1 Definition
Hashing turns a password into a fixed fingerprint that can't be reversed. A session token is a random secret proving you signed in.
2 Analogy
The bank keeps a fingerprint of your signature, not your pen. After you show ID once, you get a visitor badge (token) for the day.
3 Technical
  • PBKDF2-SHA256 repeats hashing 600,000 times with a random salt, so guessing is slow and identical passwords give different hashes.
  • Comparison is constant-time (hmac.compare_digest), so the comparison time reveals nothing about the hash.
  • The token is 32 random bytes; only SHA-256(token) is stored.
4 In JalRakshak
auth.hash_password, verify_password, _create_session, account_for_token.
5 Code
Stored format: pbkdf2_sha256$600000$<salt>$<hash>
6 Steps
  1. Login: read the salt and iterations from the stored string.
  2. Recompute the hash and compare.
  3. Create a token; store its SHA-256; return the token once.
  4. Each later request: hash the presented token, look it up, check expiry and that the account is active.
7 Mistakes
Encrypting passwords (reversible) instead of hashing. Storing raw tokens. Using random instead of secrets.
8 Vs similar
Hashing (one-way) vs encryption (two-way with a key) vs encoding (base64; no secrecy at all).
9 Practice
Why is a slow hash good here but bad for the token lookup? (Passwords have low entropy and need slowing down against guessing. Tokens are 256-bit random values that can't be guessed, so a fast SHA-256 is fine.)
10 Viva
Q: If someone steals the DB, can they sign in? A: Not directly: they get password hashes (slow to crack) and token hashes (useless as tokens).

Concept 10: Jain fairness index

1 Definition
A number from 1/n to 1 showing how equal the shares are; 1 = everyone got the same fraction of what they asked.
2 Analogy
Cutting a cake: 1.0 if every plate gets the same share of what each person wanted; low if a few plates get everything.
3 Technical
J = (Σxᵢ)² / (n·Σxᵢ²), where xᵢ = got/asked for request i.
4 In JalRakshak
FCFS 0.50, Priority 0.57, Water-aware 0.74, Round Robin 0.91 on the sample.
5 Code
m.jain_fairness = (sum_x * sum_x) / (n * sum_x2) in compute_metrics().
6 Steps (FCFS)
  • x = [1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0].
  • Σx = 4, Σx² = 4.
  • J = 16 / (8 × 4) = 0.50.
7 Mistakes
Treating fairness as the only goal. Equal shares can still be bad if the hospital needed 100%.
8 Vs similar
Jain (equality) vs importance-weighted satisfaction (equality weighted by priority) vs utilisation (how much water was used at all).
9 Practice
What is J if every request gets exactly 62.5%? (1.0: perfectly equal.)
10 Viva
Q: Why isn't Round Robin your recommended rule if it's the fairest? A: It ignores importance and deadlines. Our goal balances fairness with protecting essential users.

Phase 12 · Final project knowledge document

A compact reference, consistent with the source code (v1.0) and the earlier phases. Use it for revision; the phases above have the details.

1. Abstract

JalRakshak is a web-based decision-support system for allocating scarce reservoir water among organisations. It models each water request as a process and the supply pipeline as a CPU. It implements four scheduling policies in C++17 on a shared discrete-event engine:

  • First Come First Served;
  • non-preemptive Priority;
  • Round Robin (2,000 L quantum);
  • a new Water-aware policy, which combines five weighted factors with a guarantee floor that prevents starvation.

A FastAPI backend runs the scheduler as a child process connected by pipes. It stores each applied plan in PostgreSQL inside a single transaction that uses row-level locks, so concurrent operators can't allocate the same water twice. A Python ML pipeline forecasts demand (random forest, MAE 1,223 L / 5.1% on synthetic data) and detects possible leaks (robust z-score, precision 0.88, recall 0.66). React panels serve requesters and administrators with role-based access and an append-only audit log.

Across 600 synthetic scenarios, Water-aware left no request without water under moderate or severe scarcity. Round Robin was fairest; Priority delivered most to important users under severe scarcity.

2. Problem statement

When available water is less than requested, utilities must decide who receives how much and when. Simple rules (arrival order, strict priority) either ignore importance or starve less important users. Decisions are rarely compared, recorded, or protected against concurrent errors. Context: about 600 million Indians face high to extreme water stress (NITI Aayog CWMI 2018, as reported), and cities such as Delhi and Bengaluru supply water a few hours a day on average (2023 study).

14. Main algorithms and business logic

  • Engine:
    • clock starts after the batch window ("all" in the backend);
    • pick from the ready list;
    • grant = min(asked, left, cap); refuse if below 20% of the request;
    • burst = grant ÷ 500;
    • then rounding down to 0.01 L, invariant checks, metrics.
  • Water-aware score: 0.30·priority + 0.25·urgency (half level, half deadline pressure) + 0.15·scarcity·(1 − share) + 0.15·population + 0.15·(1 − past share).
  • Cap: max(0.3·asked, left − 0.3·others).
  • Business rules:
    • at most 5 pending requests per organisation;
    • a request can't exceed capacity;
    • sign-ups need approval;
    • 5 wrong passwords → 5-minute lock;
    • sessions last 12 h;
    • the first admin must change the password.
  • ML: robust z-score (same-weekday median, MAD, K = 3, minimum excess 15%); lag-feature models, time split of 90 days, best MAE selected, recursive 7-day forecast.
